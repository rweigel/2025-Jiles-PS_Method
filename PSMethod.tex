\begin{abstract}
The motion of a charged particle in a magnetic dipole field has no general analytical solution, but has two exact invariants: the kinetic energy and the canonical azimuthal angular momentum. 
%The magnetic moment, the first adiabatic invariant associated with the gyromotion, is conserved only approximately; although it is the basis for the guiding center approximation, its invariance over long timescales has no rigorous justification. Historically, numerical analysis of the long-time validity of magnetic moment invariance has been limited by computational speed and accuracy. 
The guiding center approximation is frequently used to understand trapped particle motion, but it is known to be valid only for limited times, and its assumptions are not satisfied for all trapped particles. Addressing these issues has historically been limited by computational speed and accuracy.
In this work, we investigate the use of the Parker--Sochacki power series (PS) method, benchmarking it against Runge--Kutta (RK) methods for this problem and using the conservation of the exact invariants as the measure of long-time accuracy and stability. Unlike the fourth-order (RK4), Dormand--Prince (RK45), and symplectic Gauss--Legendre (RKG) methods, which use derivative estimates at each time step, the PS method uses a power series expansion of the solution in time. Benchmarked at identical fixed time steps for protons and electrons over multiple energies and pitch angles, the PS method conserves the kinetic energy 4 to 13 orders of magnitude better than the RK methods, and the canonical momentum 4 to 10 orders better than the explicit RK methods and comparably to RKG. PS is the only method that is accurate and stable across all simulations considered in this study. In long-time simulations, PS errors grow approximately linearly over $10^9$ equatorial gyroperiods, whereas RKG develops secular energy error growth beyond $10^6$. When the methods are instead compared at a target relative kinetic energy error, rather than a common fixed time step, the PS method is roughly ten times faster than RK4 and twice as fast as RKG in representative runs. These results demonstrate that the PS method can be used to evaluate the long-time behavior of charged particles trapped in a dipole field, over timescales and in regimes where the validity of the guiding center approximation is unknown.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{SEC:intro}
Many physical systems can be modeled by ordinary differential equations (ODEs), yet closed-form solutions exist only for linear problems and a limited set of specially structured nonlinear problems, making numerical methods essential \cite{Chapra2021}. Space physics offers a demanding example, where the motion of a charged particle in a magnetic dipole field has no general analytical solution \cite{DragtFinn1976} yet has two exact invariants of motion: kinetic energy and canonical azimuthal angular momentum (hereafter referred to as canonical momentum). Numerical solutions of the long-time dynamics must conserve these invariants across many gyroperiods, posing an accuracy challenge. Numerical ODE solution methods such as Runge--Kutta (RK) have become standard tools \cite{Chapra2021, Butcher2016}; these advance the solution incrementally by sampling derivative estimates at each time step. The Parker--Sochacki power series (PS) method is a conceptually distinct alternative: rather than estimating derivatives at each time step, it uses a time-dependent power-series expansion of the solution specific to the system of equations. In this work, we implement the PS method for relativistic charged-particle dynamics in a static magnetic dipole field and evaluate its ability to conserve the system's exact invariants. Evaluating the exact invariants is motivated by a third quantity only approximately conserved in the dipole field: the magnetic moment, $\mu = p_{\perp}^{2}/(2mB)$, the first adiabatic invariant.

A particle in a dipole magnetic field with velocity components both parallel and perpendicular to the magnetic field will undergo multiple simultaneous periodic motions: gyration about the field line (gyro), oscillation along it (bounce), and the slow azimuthal motion across field lines (drift) \cite{Northrop1963, Dragt1965, Walt1994}. In strongly inhomogeneous magnetic fields, where all three motions are present, the adiabatic invariants are the basis for the guiding center approximation, which is valid in regimes where the gyroradius, $r_g$, remains small compared to the length scale over which the magnetic field varies, $L_B \equiv {B}/{|\nabla B|}$, such that $\varepsilon \equiv r_g/L_B \ll 1$ \cite{Northrop1963}. Their long-time behavior is central to the understanding of trapped-particle dynamics over timescales long compared to the gyroperiod \cite{DragtFinn1976}.

Although the assumption of adiabatic invariance is sufficient for addressing many problems in trapped-particle dynamics, not all problems of interest satisfy this assumption, in which case numerical integration of the exact equations of motion is required. Examples include the tracing of high-energy protons and electrons in radiation belts, where the gyroradius is no longer small compared with the scale over which the field varies \cite{Yugo2001, Yugo2007}, and the chaotic scattering of relativistic protons in the inner magnetosphere, where the motion is nonadiabatic, and its outcome depends sensitively on initial conditions \cite{Meseguer2025}. The former studies \cite{Yugo2001, Yugo2007} used a symplectic Gauss--Legendre Runge--Kutta method that bounded the energy errors over $10^7$ steps; the latter \cite{Meseguer2025} used a ninth-order Runge--Kutta method with an adaptation from \cite{Calvo2006} that enforces the invariants at each step and reported conservation of energy and canonical momentum to near machine precision over approximately $10^2$ bounces. In both cases, the conservation of the exact invariants relies on structure engineered into the integrator.

Another example is the long-time conservation of the magnetic moment itself and the implications for the guiding center approximation. A seminal analysis \cite{DragtFinn1976} concluded that the use of adiabatic invariants to predict the long-time behavior of radiation belt particles has no rigorous justification, mathematical or numerical, and expected round-off errors to defeat any numerical test. Whether the magnetic moment remains effectively conserved over long timescales is an open question.

Addressing such problems numerically places substantial demands on the integrator. In each example, trajectories must remain accurate over many gyroperiods so that numerical error is not mistaken for physical effects. For the magnetic moment, the demands are the most stringent; conservation must be demonstrated over multi-year time scales, comparable to the lifetimes of trapped radiation belt particles. The guiding center approximation cannot test what it presupposes; only the full trajectory lets the magnetic moment evolve freely. Additionally, the changes in the magnetic moment at successive equatorial crossings depend on the relative phase of the gyro and bounce motions, and their slow accumulation degrades the invariant in a way that short-time error metrics cannot detect \cite{DragtFinn1976, Dragt1965}. Therefore, the magnetic moment's evolution over time has physical meaning only if it can be distinguished from numerical drift. Preserving the exact invariants of the magnetic dipole problem requires high precision before the magnetic moment can be meaningfully studied over long timescales. This work establishes the necessary foundation with the PS method, showing that it conserves the exact invariants to high precision without auxiliary constraints, benchmarking it against standard RK-based integrators in the static magnetic dipole field. The long-time behavior of the magnetic moment itself, which this foundation is built to support, is deferred to a follow-on study. 

The remainder of this paper is organized as follows. In Section~\ref{SEC:numerical methods}, we describe the numerical methods compared in this work and the criteria of accuracy, stability, and computational efficiency used to assess them. In contrast to the RK methods, analytic calculations are required before the PS method can be applied numerically; in Appendix~\ref{sect:examples} we provide a brief introduction to the PS formalism, including three short examples of increasing complexity. In Section~\ref{sect:implementation}, we provide details on how the ODE solvers are implemented. In Section~\ref{sec:dipole}, we derive the PS recurrence relations for a charged particle in a static magnetic dipole field. In Section~\ref{SEC:results}, we benchmark the methods, first at a common fixed time step and then in a case study at a minimum target accuracy. From these results, we demonstrate that the PS method conserves the exact invariants of the static magnetic dipole problem, the kinetic energy and the canonical momentum, with higher precision than both conventional and symplectic RK methods, without auxiliary constraints and with similar computational efficiency. Section~\ref{SEC:Conclusions} summarizes our conclusions.

\section{Numerical Methods}\label{SEC:numerical methods}

In standard RK methods, derivative estimates are sampled at selected points, called stages, which are combined into a weighted average to estimate the increment in the solution at each time step. The number of stages and the chosen weights determine the order of the method. While many variations of this method exist, the fourth-order Runge--Kutta (RK4) method is the most widely used \cite{Chapra2021, Walters2022} because it provides a good balance between accuracy and computational runtime. Because RK methods approximate the solution incrementally from local derivative estimates, their step sizes are constrained by numerical stability and accuracy requirements, particularly in stiff or rapidly varying systems, where very small time steps may be necessary. For such problems, the adaptive Runge--Kutta Dormand--Prince (RK45) method, which adjusts the time step size based on a local error estimate, can be used \cite{Chapra2021, Dormand1980}. For problems where long-time stability or the preservation of physical invariants is critical, volume-preserving integrators are often used. The second-order Boris algorithm, distinct from RK methods, is the most widely used of these and shows bounded energy error over long integrations \cite{Qin2013, Ripperda2018}. However, because it is second-order, it has accuracy limitations for precise long-time particle trajectory applications \cite{Qin2013, Ripperda2018, Winkel2015}. Therefore, we include the fourth-order symplectic Gauss--Legendre Runge--Kutta (RKG) method for comparison, an implicit RK method that preserves the system's Hamiltonian structure over extended integration periods while retaining higher-order trajectory accuracy \cite{Iserles1996, Yugo2001, Calvo2003}. These comparisons are not meant to be exhaustive; higher-order RK methods \cite{Walters2022, Meseguer2025}, variations of the Boris method \cite{Wang2025}, and other improved trajectory-integration schemes \cite{Soni2021, Brizard2022, Huang2022, Umeda2023} remain active areas of research in this field, each implementing different mechanisms or constraints for maintaining accuracy and stability. Our intent is to evaluate a method that does not require such modifications but instead conserves invariants through the accuracy of the solution itself, rather than through constraints engineered into the integrator.

The Parker--Sochacki method, a power-series approach for solving initial value problems, builds on the classical Picard method \cite{Parker1996}. In the Picard method, the differential equation is first written in integral form, and the solution is approximated iteratively by substituting the previous approximation into this integral \cite{Boyce2017}. Each iteration refines the approximation from the previous one, giving a power series expansion of the exact solution when one exists. The Picard method applies to ODEs of the form $d\mathbf{x}/dt=f(\mathbf{x},t)$ and converges to the exact solution on intervals where the solution possesses a valid power series representation. In special cases, the resulting series is a known closed form; in general, the series itself is the solution. If the solution has no such expansion, the method fails to produce a solution in any form.

%Boyce2017:"One method of showing that the integral equation (3) has a unique solution is known as the method of successive approximations or Picard’s iteration method. In using this method, we start by choosing an initial function φ0, either arbitrarily or to approximate in some way the solution of the initial value problem. The simplest choice is φ0(t) = 0;  then φ0 at least satisfies the initial condition in equations (2), although presumably not the differential equation. The next approximation φ1 is obtained by substituting φ0(s) for φ (s) in the right-hand side of equation (3) and calling the result of this operation φ1(t)."

While the Picard method is theoretically powerful and offers high-order accuracy, its practical use has historically been limited by the algebraic complexity of performing successive integrations for nonlinear, nonpolynomial, or coupled ODE systems to obtain a power series representation \cite{Ansari2024, Leal2015, Rudmin1998}. The PS method addresses these limitations by using auxiliary variables and Cauchy product identities to rewrite the nonlinear components of an ODE, thereby reducing algebraic complexity and giving recursive approximations \cite{Parker1996, Guenther2019}. This approach preserves the underlying requirement that the exact solution be analytic for the series to converge, but it makes the construction of that power series tractable for a wide range of nonlinear systems. Consequently, the step size is directly linked to the convergence rate of the power series representation as illustrated in \cite{Parker1996}, enabling the use of larger, analytically informed time steps. This contrasts with RK methods, where step sizes are typically determined by numerical stability constraints and local error estimates rather than by the analytic properties of the solution.

We assess the PS method on three criteria: accuracy, stability, and computational efficiency. Accuracy is quantified by the relative errors of the exact invariants. Stability refers to the ability of a method to conserve these invariants over extended numbers of equatorial gyroperiods, without showing rapidly increasing relative errors or stalled execution. Computational efficiency is evaluated first by comparing runtimes under identical fixed-time-step conditions (fixed-step), and second by comparing the runtime required to achieve a minimum target relative kinetic energy error for select runs. The RK4 method is used as a baseline for comparison with a fixed-step PS method. A comparison with the adaptive RK45 method is used to assess the performance of the fixed-step PS approach relative to an adaptive-step solver. The fixed-step RKG is included as a fourth-order symplectic reference.

\section{Implementation of ODE Solvers}
\label{sect:implementation}
We implement the RK and PS methods in Python using 64-bit floating-point numbers \cite{IEEE754}, NumPy \cite{harris2020} arrays, and Numba \cite{lam2015} to accelerate performance-critical loops. For the PS method, each dynamical and auxiliary variable is represented as a truncated power series in a dimensionless time variable. The PS recurrence relations, explicitly derived in Section~\ref{sec:dipole}, are used to compute the power series coefficients recursively up to a maximum order of 40. In practice, an adaptive truncation criterion terminates the summation well before this order: at each step, after adding each successive order's contribution to the running sum, we compute the ratio of that contribution's magnitude to the magnitude of the accumulated partial sum for each dynamical variable, and the summation is terminated once the largest such ratio falls below a fixed tolerance (set to machine epsilon).

We compare the PS method to a fixed-step RK4 method, the adaptive-step RK45 method \cite{Dormand1980} as implemented by SciPy \cite{scipy}, and the two-stage, fourth-order, symplectic RKG method (identified as RKG2 in \cite{Yugo2007}). Following the approach of \cite{Yugo2007}, we solve the stage equations by a fixed-point iteration \cite{Calvo2003}. The iteration tolerances and limits are not reported in \cite{Yugo2001, Yugo2007}; we therefore chose values consistent with the reported step size ($65$ steps per gyroperiod) and verified them against our trajectories and the reported relative kinetic energy errors. No results were reported on canonical momentum for comparison. We iterate until the largest change in the stage values between successive sweeps falls below $10^{-15}$, capping at $100$ sweeps, though roughly 10 sweeps were typically required. A larger tolerance resulted in a secular drift not observed in \cite{Yugo2007}, whereas at $10^{-15}$ the kinetic energy error remained bounded and at levels consistent with those reported in \cite{Yugo2007} for the time interval. All fixed-step solvers (RK4, RKG, and PS) are run with the same time step, ensuring that differences in runtime and invariant conservation arise from the methods themselves rather than from step-size adaptivity; RK45, which selects its own step size, is included as an adaptive-step reference. For RK45, the relative and absolute tolerances are $10^{-8}$ and $10^{-10}$, respectively. For the PS method, the power series expansions are derived analytically; beyond rewriting the equations of motion in dimensionless form, no further time or field rescaling is used. All trajectory calculations use Cartesian coordinates.

\section{PS Method for the Dipole Problem}\label{sec:dipole}
We now apply the PS method to the motion of a relativistic charged particle in a static magnetic dipole field. The dynamics of such a particle are governed by the Lorentz force law,
        \begin{equation*}
           m_{\text{rel}}\frac{d\mathbf{v}}{dt}=q\,\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B},
        \end{equation*}
where $q$ is the particle's charge, $\mathbf{B}$ is the magnetic field, and $m_{\text{rel}}$ is the relativistic mass. The particle's position evolves according to $d\mathbf{r}/dt=\mathbf{v}$, where $\mathbf{r}$ and $\mathbf{v}$ are the three-dimensional position and velocity vectors of the particle. Since the magnetic field does no work on the particle, the scalar speed remains constant, allowing the relativistic mass to be treated as a fixed parameter.

To nondimensionalize the equations of motion, we define dimensionless position and velocity vectors by introducing a characteristic length, $r_0$, and speed,
$v_0$, such that
        \begin{equation*}
            \mathbf{r'}=\frac{\mathbf{r}}{r_0}\quad\mbox{and}\quad
            \mathbf{v'}=\frac{\mathbf{v}}{v_0}.
        \end{equation*}
We take $r_0 = R_E$, one Earth radius, and normalize the magnetic field by the equatorial field strength at that distance,
        \begin{equation*}
            B_0 = \frac{\mu_0 \mathfrak{m}}{4\pi R_E^3},
        \end{equation*}
where $\mu_0$ is the vacuum permeability and $\mathfrak{m}$ is the magnitude of the dipole moment. Time is normalized by the inverse of the angular gyrofrequency associated with $B_0$,
        \begin{equation*}
            \tau\equiv\frac{t}{\tau_0},\qquad
            \tau_0\equiv\frac{m_{\text{rel}}}{|q|B_0}\;,
        \end{equation*}
with $v_0$ chosen such that $r_0/v_0=\tau_0$. The dimensionless equations of motion are then
        \begin{align}
            \frac{d\mathbf{v'}}{d\tau} &= s\,\mathbf{v'}\times \mathbf{B'}
            \label{EQN: Rep Lorentz}\\
        \intertext{and}
            \frac{d\mathbf{r'}}{d\tau} &= \mathbf{v'},
            \label{EQN: Rep dr/dt}
        \end{align}
where $s \equiv q/|q|$ is the sign of the charge, $+1$ for ions and $-1$ for electrons, and the dimensionless dipole field is
        \begin{equation}
            \mathbf{B'}(\mathbf{r'})=\frac{\mathbf{B}}{B_0}
            =\frac{3(\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{m}}}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{r}})\hat{\mathbf{r}}
            - \hat{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{m}}}}{r'^3},
            \label{EQN: Dipole B main}
        \end{equation}
where $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{m}}}$ is the unit vector along the dipole axis. For the PS construction, we align the dipole axis with the $z$-axis, $\hat{\boldsymbol{\mathfrak{m}}} = -\hat{\mathbf{z}}$, representing a southward-pointing Earth-like dipole. In Cartesian coordinates Eq.~\eqref{EQN: Dipole B main} becomes
        \begin{equation}
            B'_x = -\frac{3x'z'}{r'^5}, \qquad
            B'_y = -\frac{3y'z'}{r'^5}, \qquad
            B'_z = -\frac{2z'^2 - x'^2 - y'^2}{r'^5},
            \label{EQN: Dipole B components}
        \end{equation}
where $r' = \sqrt{x'^2 + y'^2 + z'^2}$. 

The fixed time step is referenced to the gyroperiod at the local magnetic equator, where the field is weakest along a given field line and the gyroperiod is longest. For the field line crossing the magnetic equator at radial distance $L$ (the magnetic shell parameter in units of $R_E$), the equatorial field strength is $B_{\mathrm{eq}} = B_0/L^3$. Therefore, the dimensionless equatorial gyroperiod is
        \begin{equation*}
            T = \frac{2\pi B_0}{B_{\mathrm{eq}}} = 2\pi L^3.
        \end{equation*}
The resulting dimensionless step size is
        \begin{equation}
            \Delta \tau = \frac{2\pi L^3}{N},
            \label{eqn: dipole tau}
        \end{equation}
where $N$ is the number of integration steps per equatorial gyroperiod. Because the local gyroperiod scales inversely with the field strength, the off-equatorial portions of a trajectory are resolved by proportionally fewer steps per gyration; the shortest local gyroperiod occurs at the mirror points, where $B = B_{\mathrm{eq}}/\sin^2\alpha_{\mathrm{eq}}$ for equatorial pitch angle $\alpha_{\mathrm{eq}}$, so for the smallest pitch angle considered here ($30^\circ$) every gyration is resolved by no fewer than $N/4$ steps.


\subsection{Recurrence Relations}
From this point forward, all variables are understood to be dimensionless, and the prime notation is omitted. We begin by expressing the $x$-component of position and velocity as power series in time:
        \begin{equation}\label{EQN: ConstB x}
            x=\sum_{i=0}^\infty x_i\tau^i
        \end{equation}
        \begin{equation}\label{EQN: ConstB v}
            v_x=\sum_{i=0}^\infty v_{xi}\tau^i .
        \end{equation}
Substituting Eqs.~\eqref{EQN: ConstB x} and \eqref{EQN: ConstB v} into the
$x$-component of Eq.~\eqref{EQN: Rep dr/dt} gives
    \begin{align*}
            \frac{d}{d\tau}\left(\sum_{i=0}^\infty x_i\tau^i\right)=& \sum_{i=0}^\infty v_{xi}\tau^i\;,\\
\intertext{which reduces to}
            \sum_{i=0}^\infty ix_i\tau^{i-1}=&\sum_{i=0}^\infty v_{xi}\tau^i.
    \end{align*}
We reindex the left-hand side using $i \to i+1$ and equate powers of $\tau$ giving
    \begin{equation*}
        x_{i+1}=\frac{v_{xi}}{i+1}.
    \end{equation*}
This generates the coefficients for Eq.~\eqref{EQN: ConstB x}. Similarly, the remaining position recurrence relations are
        \begin{align*}
            y_{i+1}=\frac{v_{yi}}{i+1}\quad
            \mbox{and}\quad
            z_{i+1}=\frac{v_{zi}}{i+1}.
        \end{align*}
The remaining recurrence relations rely on two power series identities. Products of series are combined using the Cauchy product identity,
        \begin{equation}
            \left(\sum_{i=0}^\infty P_i\tau^i\right)\left(\sum_{i=0}^\infty Q_i\tau^i\right)
            =\sum_{i=0}^\infty\tau^i\sum_{j=0}^i P_jQ_{i-j}.
            \label{EQN:cauchyb}
        \end{equation}
For the ratio of two power series, $W=P/Q$, rearranging as $QW=P$ and applying Eq.~\eqref{EQN:cauchyb} to the left-hand side gives
    \begin{align*}
        \sum_{j=0}^i Q_jW_{i-j}=&\,P_i.\\
\intertext{Isolating the $j=0$ term from the summation gives}
        Q_0W_i+\sum_{j=1}^i Q_jW_{i-j}=&\,P_i,
    \end{align*}
and rearranging results in the identity
        \begin{equation}
            W_i=\frac{1}{Q_0}\left(P_i-\sum_{j=1}^i Q_jW_{i-j}\right).
            \label{EQN: Cauchy a/b}
        \end{equation}
We define a set of auxiliary variables ($a,\,b,\,c,\,d,\,e,\,f,\,g,$ and $u$) by first letting
        \begin{align*}
            a &\equiv \frac{1}{r^5}, \\
            b &\equiv 2z^2-x^2-y^2, \\
            c &\equiv yz,\\
         \intertext{and}
            d &\equiv xz,
        \end{align*}
such that
        \begin{align*}
           B_x&=-\frac{1}{r^5}3xz=-3ad,\\
           B_y&=-\frac{1}{r^5}3yz=-3ac,\\
         \intertext{and}
            B_z&=-\frac{1}{r^5}(2z^2-x^2-y^2)=-ab.
        \end{align*}
Next, we express the acceleration equations in terms of these auxiliary variables and define the remaining auxiliary variables,
        \begin{align*}
            \frac{dv_x}{d\tau} &= s\left(B_z v_y - B_y v_z\right)
            = sa(3c v_z - b v_y) = sae, \\
            \frac{dv_y}{d\tau} &= s\left(B_x v_z - B_z v_x\right)
            = sa(b v_x - 3d v_z) = saf, \\
         \intertext{and}
            \frac{dv_z}{d\tau} &= s\left(B_y v_x - B_x v_y\right)
            = sa(3d v_y - 3c v_x) = sag,
        \end{align*}
where
        \begin{align*}
            e &\equiv 3c v_z - b v_y, \\
            f &\equiv b v_x - 3d v_z, \\
         \intertext{and}
            g &\equiv 3d v_y - 3c v_x.
        \end{align*}
Let $u\equiv r^2$ such that
        \begin{gather*}
            	    a=u^{-\frac{5}{2}},\\
            \intertext{and using}
            	\ln a=-\frac{5}{2}\ln u\\
             \intertext{gives}
            	\frac{1}{a}\frac{da}{d\tau}=-\frac{5}{2}\frac{1}{u}\frac{du}{d\tau}\\
            \intertext{which can be rearranged into an expression containing $a/u$, a series divided by another series,}
            		\frac{da}{d\tau}=-\frac{5}{2}\frac{a}{u}\frac{du}{d\tau}.\\
            \intertext{Letting $\zeta=a/u$, substituting in the summations for each auxiliary variable, and reindexing the derivative terms gives }
            		\sum_{i=0}^\infty (i+1)a_{i+1}\tau^i=-\frac{5}{2}\left( \sum_{i=0}^\infty\zeta_i\tau^i \right)\left( \sum_{i=0}^\infty (i+1)u_{i+1}\tau^i \right), \\
            \intertext{where the terms on the right can be simplified using the Cauchy product identity, Eq.~\eqref{EQN:cauchyb},}
                    	\sum_{i=0}^\infty (i+1)a_{i+1}\tau^i=-\frac{5}{2}\sum_{i=0}^\infty \tau^i\left(\sum_{j=0}^i(i-j+1)\zeta_j u_{i-j+1}\right).\\
            \intertext{Matching powers of $\tau$ gives}
                    a_{i+1}=-\frac{5}{2(i+1)}\sum_{j=0}^i(i-j+1)\zeta_j u_{i-j+1},\\
            \intertext{and from Eq.~\eqref{EQN: Cauchy a/b}, with $P=a$ and $Q=u$, we have}
                \zeta_i=\frac{1}{u_0}\left(a_i-\sum_{j=1}^iu_j\zeta_{i-j}\right)\quad\mbox{where}\quad \zeta_0=\frac{a_0}{u_0}.
        \end{gather*}
Applying the same process, the recurrence relations for the remaining auxiliary variables are
        \begin{align*}
            u_i=&\sum_{j=0}^i x_jx_{i-j}+\sum_{j=0}^i y_jy_{i-j}+\sum_{j=0}^i z_jz_{i-j},\\
            b_i=&\,2\sum_{j=0}^i z_jz_{i-j}-\sum_{j=0}^i x_jx_{i-j}-\sum_{j=0}^i y_jy_{i-j},\\
            c_i=&\sum_{j=0}^i y_jz_{i-j},\\
            d_i=&\sum_{j=0}^i x_jz_{i-j},\\
            e_i=&\,3\sum_{j=0}^i c_jv_{z,i-j}-\sum_{j=0}^i b_jv_{y,i-j},\\
            f_i=&\sum_{j=0}^i b_jv_{x,i-j}-3\sum_{j=0}^i d_jv_{z,i-j},\\
    \intertext{and}
            g_i=&\,3\sum_{j=0}^i d_jv_{y,i-j}-3\sum_{j=0}^i c_jv_{x,i-j}.
        \end{align*}
The velocity recurrence relations can then be expressed as
        \begin{align*}
            v_{x, i+1} &=  \frac{s}{i+1} \sum_{j=0}^i a_j e_{i-j}, \\
            v_{y, i+1} &=   \frac{s}{i+1} \sum_{j=0}^i a_j f_{i-j}, \\
      \intertext{and}
            v_{z, i+1} &=   \frac{s}{i+1} \sum_{j=0}^i a_j g_{i-j}.
        \end{align*}
At each time step, the zeroth-order coefficients ($x_0$, $v_{x0}$, $u_0$, and so on) are evaluated from the state at the beginning of the step, and the recurrence relations above then generate all higher-order coefficients. The updated state follows from evaluating the truncated series at $\tau = \Delta\tau$. At the end of each step, the auxiliary variables are re-evaluated, or tethered, directly from the advanced position and velocity rather than advanced by their own summed series, keeping them consistent with the dynamical state at the start of the next step (see Appendix~\ref{Intro: ExIII} for details).

\section{Results}\label{SEC:results}

\subsection{Comparisons at a Common Fixed Time Step}
For the following simulations, $N=65$ is used for each fixed-step method, corresponding to 65 integration steps per gyroperiod at the magnetic equator, where the gyroperiod is longest. Particles are initialized at $5\,R_E$, giving a fixed time step, from Eq.~\eqref{eqn: dipole tau}, of $\Delta\tau=2\pi(5^3)/65\approx12.1$. Simulation durations are expressed as the number of elapsed equatorial gyroperiods, $\tau/T$. Initially, simulations were run for $\tau/T=10^{6}$; however, both RK4 and RK45 frequently became unstable due to high kinetic energy errors. Additionally, RK45 underwent repeated step-size refinements that led to excessive computational runtime and, in several instances, stalling execution. To allow for meaningful comparison and to capture the long-time trends of the RK methods, each simulation was run for a common time interval of $\tau/T = 1.5\times 10^{5}$. This allowed most RK4 and RK45 runs to complete before instability (see Tables~\ref{tab:proton_energy_results} and \ref{tab:electron_energy_results} in Appendix~\ref{appendix:tables}). Additional long-time analyses using extended gyroperiod counts were performed for the PS and RKG methods and are discussed in the corresponding subsections. 

Accuracy is defined by the relative errors of the two exact invariants, kinetic energy, $E$, and canonical momentum, $P_{\phi}$. The relative kinetic energy error is
        \begin{equation*}
            \frac{|\Delta E|}{E_0}=\frac{|E-E_0|}{E_0}
            \quad\mbox{where}\quad
            E = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(v_x^2+v_y^2+v_z^2\right),
        \end{equation*}
with $E$ the dimensionless kinetic energy per unit relativistic mass and $E_0$ its initial value. The relative canonical momentum error,
        \begin{equation*}
            \frac{|\Delta P_{\phi}|}{|P_{\phi,0}|}=\frac{|P_\phi-P_{\phi,0}|}{|P_{\phi,0}|}
            \quad\mbox{where}\quad
            P_\phi = \rho\, v_\phi - s\,\frac{\rho^2}{r^3},
        \end{equation*}
and $\rho = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2}$ is the cylindrical radius, $r = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}$, $v_\phi = (x v_y - y v_x)/\rho$ is the azimuthal velocity, $s = q/|q|$ is the sign of the charge, and $P_{\phi,0}$ is the initial canonical momentum. Since $\gamma$ is constant in this formulation, conservation of $E$ is equivalent to conservation of the speed, and hence of the relativistic kinetic energy. For a small drift in speed, the corresponding relative drift in the relativistic kinetic energy exceeds that of $E$ by a factor of $\gamma(\gamma+1)/2$; because this factor depends only on the particle and not on the integrator, it is common to all methods for a given run and does not affect any comparison between them.

For the dipole field, the gradient scale length at the equatorial crossing of the field line is $L_B = L/3$, and the dimensionless equatorial gyroradius is $r_g = v_{\perp} L^{3}$, where $v_{\perp}$ is the dimensionless perpendicular speed at the equator. The parameter $\varepsilon$ introduced in Section~\ref{SEC:intro}, evaluated at the equator, is therefore
        \begin{equation*}
            \varepsilon = \frac{r_g}{L_B} = 3\, v_{\perp} L^{2}.
        \end{equation*}
Across the simulations discussed below, $\varepsilon$ ranges from ${\sim}3\times10^{-3}$ to ${\sim}0.2$; the highest-energy runs of both species therefore lie in a regime where the gyroradius is no longer small compared with the length scale over which the field varies, and the validity of the guiding center approximation, while not used here, is uncertain.

For the PS method, the mean truncation order is used to label each run; for example, a simulation truncated on average at the 13th order is denoted PS13.

\subsection{A Case Study at a Target Accuracy}\label{SEC:Dip Discussion}
The previous simulations used identical time steps for the PS method and the fixed-step RK methods (RK4 and RKG), enabling direct, controlled comparisons. Under this configuration, RK4 generally had shorter runtimes at the expense of substantially larger relative kinetic energy and canonical momentum errors, while the PS method produced significantly higher accuracy with correspondingly longer runtimes; the PS method typically required up to four times the computational runtime of RK4 and runtimes comparable to RKG, as summarized in Tables~\ref{tab:proton_energy_results} and~\ref{tab:electron_energy_results}. As noted in Section~\ref{SEC:intro}, the PS step size is linked to the convergence rate of the power series representation, enabling the use of larger time steps. This motivates an alternative comparison in which PS, RK4, and RKG are instead run to meet a target relative kinetic energy error with time steps chosen as large as practical, allowing computational efficiency to be assessed on the basis of a minimum accuracy requirement rather than a common step size. Revisiting the 100~keV proton at a pitch angle of $30^\circ$, if a relative kinetic energy error below $10^{-5}$ is required over four drift periods, the PS method achieves this with a fixed time step of $\Delta\tau=54$, at which the mean truncation order rises to 22 (PS22); the RK4 method requires a much smaller step of $\Delta\tau=0.5$, and the RKG method a step of $\Delta\tau=7.9$. Under this comparison, the PS method completes the simulation approximately ten times faster than RK4 and twice as fast as RKG. 

The corresponding relative errors of the exact invariants are shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:maxerror}: kinetic energy in panel~(a) and canonical momentum in panel~(b). Despite its much larger time step, PS22 has a relative kinetic energy error 5 orders of magnitude lower than those of the RK runs, and a relative canonical momentum error 2 orders of magnitude lower than that of RK4 and comparable to that of RKG. Although all methods are plotted on the same time axis, the first data points occur at different times due to the differing step sizes, resulting in an offset at the beginning of each curve. An analogous comparison for the electron simulations, using the PS and RK4 methods, gives the same qualitative result.

Note that the 5 orders of magnitude difference in kinetic energy error between the PS method and the RK methods reflects a property of the PS method: increasing the step size raises the truncation order selected by the adaptive criterion while maintaining accuracy. Forcing the PS error up to the target accuracy required terminating the truncation early at an arbitrary fixed order, which increased the error without appreciably reducing runtime. Unlike the RK methods, whose error can be adjusted by the step size, the PS method derives its efficiency gains from the larger time steps the series representation supports rather than from sacrificing accuracy. Conversely, forcing the RK methods to errors comparable to the PS method's required thousands of steps per gyroperiod, with runtimes tens to hundreds of times slower than the PS method.

The results show that the PS method can meet the accuracy requirement with significantly larger time steps, allowing computational gains when stringent accuracy criteria define solution requirements for a given time interval. Although computational runtime was not the primary objective of this study, these findings indicate that, with appropriate parameter selection, the PS method can be not only accurate but also competitive or superior in efficiency for charged-particle applications. Adopting additional methods for adaptive order and step-size control could further improve these results. 

\section{Conclusions}\label{SEC:Conclusions}
This study demonstrates that the Parker--Sochacki power series (PS) method provides a highly accurate and stable framework for integrating charged-particle motion in a magnetic dipole field. As discussed in Section~\ref{SEC:intro}, the long-time evolution of the magnetic moment has physical meaning only if it can be distinguished from numerical drift; by conserving the exact invariants to high precision up to $10^9$ equatorial gyroperiods, including for high-energy particles whose gyroradii are no longer small compared with the scale length over which the field varies, the PS method provides the numerical foundation such an analysis requires. Additionally, conservation of the invariants was accomplished without introducing  auxiliary constraints or engineering conservation into the method. This establishes it as a viable tool for evaluating the long-time behavior of the adiabatic invariants on timescales and in regimes where their conservation is not guaranteed by adiabatic theory. Although developed here in support of that goal, the precision of this framework extends naturally to other space physics applications with similarly stringent accuracy requirements, such as cutoff-rigidity and magnetospheric access calculations, where trajectories must remain physically accurate over many gyroperiods.

These results establish the PS method as an alternative to traditional ODE solvers for charged-particle motion in magnetic fields, particularly for long-time, high-precision analyses. Benchmarked at identical fixed time steps against established RK methods (RK4, RK45, and the symplectic RKG), the PS method preserved the exact invariants of the problem to high precision, with relative kinetic energy errors 4 to 13 orders of magnitude lower than those of the RK methods and canonical momentum errors 4 to 10 orders of magnitude lower than those of the explicit RK methods, comparable to, and typically within an order of magnitude below, those of RKG. A further insight from this study is that the symplectic structure of the RKG method does not guarantee bounded energy error over arbitrarily long integrations of the static magnetic dipole problem. Although its kinetic energy error is initially bounded, extended integrations reveal the eventual onset of relative error growth. By contrast, the PS method's energy error grows approximately linearly but at a significantly smaller magnitude. 

As noted in Section~\ref{sect:implementation}, wall-clock computation times indicate the relative behavior of the methods rather than definitive rankings, and conclusions regarding computational efficiency depend on the basis of comparison. At identical fixed time steps, the PS method has longer runtimes than RK4, but with relative kinetic energy errors several orders of magnitude smaller; because RK4 is the most computationally efficient fixed-step baseline considered here, step-matched comparisons understate the efficiency of the PS method when accuracy is the controlling constraint. When the methods are instead compared at a minimum target relative kinetic energy error, the PS method meets the target with substantially larger time steps; in representative proton and electron cases, it completes the simulations with runtimes up to an order of magnitude shorter than those of the RK methods. Finally, these comparisons are sensitive to implementation choices such as iteration tolerances and starting values. We found that such information is under-reported in the available literature, requiring reconstruction from reported step size or error levels. For this reason, the complete implementation of every integrator used in this study is openly available.

\section{Examples - The Power Series Method}
\label{sect:examples}
In the PS formulation, all time-dependent variables are represented as power series expansions with recursively generated coefficients. We distinguish between two types of quantities: (i) dynamical variables, written without indexed subscripts (e.g., $x$, $v$), which are time-dependent variables governed directly by the ODE, and (ii) indexed quantities (e.g., $x_i$, $v_i$), which are constant coefficients of $t^i$ in the power series expansion that are solved for via recursion. Auxiliary variables are additional dynamical variables introduced to rewrite nonlinear functions into forms that allow for a recursion formula to be found \cite{Parker1996, Rudmin1998}.

We demonstrate the fundamentals of the method with three simple ODEs. Example I (Section \ref{Intro: ExI}) introduces the basic PS method for a 1-D ODE, $dy/dx=y$, where no auxiliary variables are needed, and it generates the exact series solution. Example II (Section \ref{Intro: ExII}) is a nonlinear 1-D ODE, $dy/dx=y^2$, and an auxiliary variable is used to linearize it and allow the exact solution to be determined with a recurrence relation. Example III (Section \ref{Intro: ExIII}) is a 2-D nonlinear ODE, and the PS method generates an approximate solution using an auxiliary variable and truncating the resulting power series. These examples establish the foundation for applying the PS method to the magnetic dipole field in Section~\ref{sec:dipole}.

\subsection{Example I - No Auxiliary Variables}\label{Intro: ExI}

To first demonstrate the PS method with no auxiliary variables, consider the ODE
    \begin{equation}\label{EQN: ExI dy/dx}
        \frac{dy}{dx}=y\quad\mbox{with}\quad y(0) =1
    \end{equation}
and express $y$ as a power series with unknown constant coefficients $y_i$,
    \begin{equation}\label{EQN: ExI y}
            y=\sum_{i=0}^\infty y_ix^i,
    \end{equation}
for which the derivative with respect to $x$ is
    \begin{equation}\label{EQN: ExI dy/dxi}
        \frac{dy}{dx}=\sum_{i=0}^\infty y_iix^{i-1}.
    \end{equation}
We see that $y_0=y(0)$ follows from Eq.~\eqref{EQN: ExI y}. Substituting Eqs. \eqref{EQN: ExI y} and \eqref{EQN: ExI dy/dxi} into Eq.~\eqref{EQN: ExI dy/dx} gives
    \begin{equation*}
            \sum_{i=0}^\infty y_iix^{i-1}=\sum_{i=0}^\infty y_ix^i.\\
    \end{equation*}
The recurrence relation is derived by first equating common powers of $x$ by reindexing the left-hand side using $i \to i+1$ giving
    \begin{equation*}
        \sum_{i=0}^\infty y_{i+1}(i+1)x^{i}=\sum_{i=0}^\infty y_ix^i.
    \end{equation*}
Hereafter, we will refer to this step simply as ``reindexing.''  Equating powers of $x$ gives
    \begin{equation}\label{EQN: ExI yi}
        y_{i+1} = \frac{y_i}{(i+1)}.
    \end{equation}
The first three coefficients are
    \begin{equation*}
        y_1=1,\quad\quad y_2=\frac{1}{2},\quad\mbox{and}\quad y_3=\frac{1}{6}.
    \end{equation*}
The general solution to Eq.~\eqref{EQN: ExI y}, using coefficients determined by Eq.~\eqref{EQN: ExI yi}, is thus
    \begin{equation*}
        y=1+x+\frac{x^2}{2!}+\frac{x^3}{3!}+\dots=e^x,
    \end{equation*}
which is the exact solution of Eq.~\eqref{EQN: ExI dy/dx}.

\subsection{Example II - Auxiliary Variable}\label{Intro: ExII}
The previous example demonstrates the use of a power series, but an auxiliary variable was not needed. Next, consider the nonlinear ODE
    \begin{equation}\label{EQN: ExII dydx}
        \frac{dy}{dx}=y^2\quad\mbox{with}\quad y(0) =1.
    \end{equation}
First, express $y$ as a power series,
    \begin{equation}\label{EQN: ExII y}
        y=\sum_{i=0}^\infty y_ix^i,   
    \end{equation}
where we see that $y_0=y(0)$. An auxiliary variable, 
    \begin{equation*}
           u \equiv y^2 = \left(\sum_{i=0}^\infty y_i x^i\right)^2,
    \end{equation*} 
can be simplified using the Cauchy product identity \cite{Knuth1997},
    \begin{equation}\label{eqn:cauchy}
        \left(\sum_{i=0}^\infty a_ix^i\right)\left(\sum_{j=0}^\infty b_jx^j\right)=\sum_{k=0}^\infty x^k\sum_{l=0}^{k} a_lb_{k-l},
    \end{equation}
giving
    \begin{equation}\label{EQN: ExII u}   
           u=\sum_{i=0}^\infty  x^i \left(\sum_{j=0}^i y_j y_{i-j}\right).
    \end{equation}      
Taking the derivative of Eq.~\eqref{EQN: ExII y} with respect to $x$ and reindexing gives  
    \begin{equation}\label{EQN: ExII dydxb}
        \frac{dy}{dx}=\sum_{i=0}^\infty (i+1)y_{i+1}x^i.
    \end{equation}
Equating Eq.~\eqref{EQN: ExII u} and Eq.~\eqref{EQN: ExII dydxb} gives
    \begin{equation*}
        \sum_{i=0}^\infty (i+1)y_{i+1}x^i=\sum_{i=0}^\infty x^i\left(\sum_{j=0}^iy_jy_{i-j}\right). 
        \end{equation*}
Equating powers of $x$ gives the recurrence relation,
    \begin{equation}
        y_{i+1}=\frac{1}{(i+1)}\left(\sum_{j=0}^iy_jy_{i-j}\right).\label{EQN: ExII yi}
    \end{equation}
The first three terms are
    \begin{align*}
            y_1=y_0y_0=&1,\\
            y_2=\frac{1}{2}\left(y_0y_1+y_1y_0\right)=&1,\\
     \intertext{and}    
            y_3=\frac{1}{3}\left(y_0y_2+y_1y_1+y_2y_0 \right)=&1.
    \end{align*}
By inspection, the general solution to Eq.~\eqref{EQN: ExII y}, using the coefficients determined by Eq. ~\eqref{EQN: ExII yi}, is 
    \begin{equation*}
        y=1+x+x^2+x^3+\dots=\frac{1}{1-x}\,,
    \end{equation*}
which is the exact solution of Eq.~\eqref{EQN: ExII dydx}. 

\subsection{Example III - ODE Solver}\label{Intro: ExIII}
The previous two examples demonstrated how the PS method constructs an exact power series solution and how auxiliary variables can be used to rewrite nonlinear terms to enable finding recurrence relations for the power series coefficients. While these problems could also be solved using the Picard method, they were selected to demonstrate the fundamental steps of the PS method. In these examples, the method directly produced closed-form power series exact solutions. However, most systems do not have a closed-form solution, and the Picard method becomes impractical due to the complexity of the algebraic expressions \cite{Ansari2024, Leal2015, Rudmin1998}. In this case, the PS method can be used as a numerical ODE solver by truncating the power series based on the number of meaningful contributing terms, defined as the maximum order for which the next term’s contribution to the sum is less than a defined tolerance, such as machine epsilon. This series is evaluated with a chosen time-step size of $\Delta t$ that determines how far in time the truncated series is used before it is reinitialized. When advanced iteratively, auxiliary variables can accumulate small numerical inconsistencies due to finite precision and truncation errors, leading to gradual deviation from their exact values. 

At the start of the next iteration, an additional stabilization strategy known as tethering \cite{Stewart2009} can be used to control the long-time accumulation of numerical error in the auxiliary variables. Tethering reinitializes the auxiliary variable at the end of each iteration to its analytical definition.

To demonstrate the setup of using the PS method for numerical ODE integration and tethering, we consider a system of two coupled ODEs corresponding to the one-dimensional motion of an object with quadratic drag:
    \begin{align}
            \frac{dx}{dt}=&\,v,\label{EQN: ExIII dx} \\
            \frac{dv}{dt}=&-v^2\nonumber.
    \end{align}
We define the auxiliary variable $u\equiv v^2$. The variables $x$, $v$, and $u$ as power series in $t$ are
    \begin{gather}
        x=\sum_{i=0}^\infty x_it^i \label{EQN: ExIII x},\\
        v=\sum_{i=0}^\infty v_it^i \label{EQN: ExIII v},\\
     \intertext{and}    
        u=\sum_{i=0}^\infty u_it^i  \label{EQN: ExIII u}.
    \end{gather}
From these power series, it follows that $x_0=x(0)$, $v_0=v(0)$, and $u_0=u(0)=v_0^2$. Taking the derivative of Eq.~\eqref{EQN: ExIII x} with respect to $t$ gives
    \begin{equation*}
        \frac{dx}{dt}=\sum_{i=0}^\infty ix_{i}t^{i-1}.
    \end{equation*}
Substituting these results and Eq.~\eqref{EQN: ExIII v} into Eq.~\eqref{EQN: ExIII dx}, and subsequently reindexing, gives  
    \begin{equation*}\label{EQN: ExIII sumvsumx}
        \sum_{i=0}^\infty (i+1)x_{i+1}t^{i}=\sum_{i=0}^\infty v_it^i  
    \end{equation*}
and equating powers of $t$ gives
    \begin{equation}\label{EQN: ExIII xi}
        x_{i+1}= \frac{v_i}{(i+1)}. 
    \end{equation}
Following the same process for Eq.~\eqref{EQN: ExIII v} gives
    \begin{equation}\label{EQN: ExIII vi}
        v_{i+1}= -\frac{u_i}{(i+1)}. 
    \end{equation}
For the final recurrence relation, note that
    \begin{align*}
        \frac{du}{dt}=2v\frac{dv}{dt}=-2vu.
    \end{align*}
Using this in the derivative of Eq.~\eqref{EQN: ExIII u} with respect to $t$ gives 
    \begin{equation*}
         \sum_{i=0}^\infty iu_it^{i-1}=\frac{du}{dt}=-2vu. 
    \end{equation*} 
Using Eqs. \eqref{EQN: ExIII v} and \eqref{EQN: ExIII u} gives
    \begin{equation*}
         \sum_{i=0}^\infty iu_it^{i-1}=-2\left( \sum_{i=0}^\infty v_it^i \right)\left( \sum_{i=0}^\infty u_it^i\right).
    \end{equation*}
Using the Cauchy product identity, Eq.~\eqref{eqn:cauchy}, gives
   \begin{equation*}
         \sum_{i=0}^\infty iu_it^{i-1}=-2\sum_{i=0}^\infty t^i\left(\sum_{j=0}^i v_ju_{i-j}\right).
    \end{equation*}
Reindexing the left-hand side and equating common powers of $t$ gives
    \begin{equation}\label{EQN: ExIII ui}
         u_{i+1}=-\frac{2}{i+1}\sum_{j=0}^iv_ju_{i-j}.
    \end{equation}
The recurrence relations, Eqs. \eqref{EQN: ExIII xi}, \eqref{EQN: ExIII vi}, and \eqref{EQN: ExIII ui}, can be substituted into Eqs. \eqref{EQN: ExIII x}, \eqref{EQN: ExIII v}, and \eqref{EQN: ExIII u}. Using the initial conditions and reindexing gives
    \begin{gather} \label{EQN: ExIII fulleqns}
        x(t)= x_0 + \sum_{i=0}^\infty\frac{v_i}{(i+1)} t^{i+1},\quad
        v(t)= v_0 - \sum_{i=0}^\infty \frac{u_i}{(i+1)}t^{i+1}, \quad\mbox{and}\quad        
        u(t)=u_0 - \sum_{i=0}^\infty \frac{2t^{i+1}}{i+1}\sum_{j=0}^iv_ju_{i-j}.
    \end{gather}

Although the relations in Eq.~\eqref{EQN: ExIII fulleqns} are expressed as an infinite series, a finite summation is needed for numerical calculations. The summations are terminated at an index denoted by the PS order, $M$. The PS order may be specified as a fixed value or set at each iteration by a criterion based on the significance of successive terms, for example, when additional contributions to the sum are less than a prescribed tolerance.

The recurrence relations in Eqs.~\eqref{EQN: ExIII xi}--\eqref{EQN: ExIII ui} were derived for the power series solution and were therefore written without iteration superscripts. When the PS method is used as a numerical ODE solver, these relations are applied at each time step $k$ to generate a new set of coefficients via
    \begin{gather}
        x_{i+1}^{(k)}=\frac{v_i^{(k)}}{i+1}, \label{eqn:EXIII xi ODE solver} \\
        v_{i+1}^{(k)}=-\frac{u_i^{(k)}}{i+1}, \label{eqn:EXIII vi ODE solver} \\
        u_{i+1}^{(k)}=-\frac{2}{i+1}\sum_{j=0}^i v_j^{(k)}u_{i-j}^{(k)}\label{eqn:EXIII ui ODE solver}\thinspace.
    \end{gather}
Similarly, the quantities $(x_{0},v_{0},u_{0})$ that previously denoted the values at $t=0$ now become iteration-dependent initial conditions $(x_{0}^{(k)},v_{0}^{(k)},u_{0}^{(k)})$, corresponding to the values at the beginning of each time step. These coefficients are then substituted into the truncated power series from Eq.~\eqref{EQN: ExIII fulleqns}, giving
       \begin{gather}
        x^{(k)}(\Delta t) \approx x_0^{(k)} + \sum_{i=0}^{M} \frac{v_i^{(k)}}{i+1}\,\Delta t^{i+1}, \label{eqn:EXIII x ODE solver}\\
        v^{(k)}(\Delta t) \approx v_0^{(k)} - \sum_{i=0}^{M} \frac{u_i^{(k)}}{i+1}\,\Delta t^{i+1}, \label{eqn:EXIII v ODE solver}\\
\intertext{and}
        u^{(k)}(\Delta t) \approx u_0^{(k)} - \sum_{i=0}^{M} \frac{2\Delta t^{i+1}}{i+1}
        \left(\sum_{j=0}^{i} v_j^{(k)}\,u_{i-j}^{(k)}\right).\label{eqn:EXIII u ODE solver}
    \end{gather}
The values $\big(x^{(k)}(\Delta t),\,v^{(k)}(\Delta t),\,u^{(k)}(\Delta t)\big)$ define the state at iteration $k$. These values are used as the initial conditions, $\big(x_0^{(k+1)},\, v_0^{(k+1)}, \,u_0^{(k+1)}\big)$, for the next iteration, $k+1$, thereby producing an iterative numerical ODE solver. Recall that a non-indexed symbol such as $x(t)$, $v(t)$, or $u(t)$  denotes a time-dependent variable in the ODE, whereas a subscripted quantity  such as $x_i$, $v_i$, or $u_i$ refers to the constant coefficient of $t^i$ in the power series expansion, and a superscript such as $x_0^{(k)}$, $v_0^{(k)}$, or $u_0^{(k)}$
denotes the value of the variable at iteration $k$.

After each iteration, the dynamical variables $x$ and $v$ are updated using recurrence relationships. The auxiliary variable $u=v^2$ can be updated in two ways. We can either use $u^{(k)}$ as the initial condition $u_0^{(k+1)}$ for the next iteration or we can use $u_0^{(k+1)}\simeq(v^{(k)}(\Delta t ))^2$, its analytical definition updated with the dynamical variable $v$. The latter choice, tethering, reinitializes the auxiliary variable to its exact value. To summarize, initial conditions when advancing from iteration $k$ to $k+1$ can be done in two ways:\\

\noindent
\begin{minipage}{0.48\textwidth}
\textbf{No Tethering}
    \begin{align*}
        x_0^{(k+1)} &= x^{(k)}\\
        v_0^{(k+1)} &= v^{(k)}\\
        u_0^{(k+1)} &= u^{(k)}
    \end{align*}
\emph{For each variable, the new initial condition is the last value from its truncated sum.\\}
\end{minipage}\hfill
\begin{minipage}{0.48\textwidth}
\textbf{Tethering}
    \begin{align*}
        x_0^{(k+1)} &= x^{(k)}\\
        v_0^{(k+1)} &= v^{(k)}\\
        u_0^{(k+1)} &= \left(v^{(k)}\right)^2
    \end{align*}
\emph{Auxiliary variable, $u$, reinitialized using its definition, $u=v^2$. For dynamical variables, the new initial condition is the last value from the truncated sum.}
\end{minipage}\\
\newline


To demonstrate the step-wise use of the recurrence relations, consider iteration $k=1$ and let $M=1$ and $\Delta t=1$.  From Eqs. \eqref{eqn:EXIII xi ODE solver}-\eqref{eqn:EXIII ui ODE solver}, the coefficient updates become
    \begin{gather*}
        x_{1}^{(1)}=v_0^{(1)}, \\
        v_{1}^{(1)}=-u_0^{(1)},  \\
\intertext{and}
        u_{1}^{(1)}=-2v_0^{(1)}u_{0}^{(1)}.
    \end{gather*}
Applying these to Eqs. \eqref{eqn:EXIII x ODE solver}-\eqref{eqn:EXIII u ODE solver}, we obtain
    \begin{gather*}
        x^{(1)}(1) \approx x_0^{(1)} + v_0^{(1)}
        - \tfrac12\big(v_0^{(1)}\big)^{2},\\
        v^{(1)}(1) \approx v_0^{(1)} - u_0^{(1)} - \tfrac12 u_1^{(1)},\\
\intertext{and}
        u^{(1)}(1) \approx  u_0^{(1)} - 2v_0^{(1)}u_0^{(1)} - v_0^{(1)}u_1^{(1)} - v_1^{(1)}u_0^{(1)}.
    \end{gather*}
This completes the first PS iteration and provides the values that become the initial conditions for iteration $k=2$ under either tethered, $u_0^{(2)}=(v^{(1)})^2$, or untethered, $u_0^{(2)}=u^{(1)}$, updating of the auxiliary variable.
